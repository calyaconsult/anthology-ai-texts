**Prompt:** Generate a piece of prose that shows specificity of memory, the irreducibly weird personal fact, the non-transferable scar.

## The Lighter

My mother used to hold ice cubes against her wrists when she was angry. Not because anyone told her to. Not because she'd read it in a magazine. She just did it, standing at the kitchen sink with her back to whoever had ruined the evening, letting the melt run down her fingers into the drain. She never explained it. I was nine before I understood it was strange.

---

The scar on my left knee is not from falling off a bicycle. Everyone assumes it's from falling off a bicycle, and for years I let them, because the truth is so small and ugly and particular that it resists telling.

I was twelve. I was kneeling on the bathroom floor trying to light a piece of notebook paper on fire with my father's Zippo—not for any reason I could have articulated, not pyromania, not a cry for help, nothing so legible. I just wanted to see the blue lines disappear. The way the ruled paper burned was satisfying to me in a way that I knew, even then, revealed something I should keep private. The flame crawled along the lines as though erasing them, and I held the paper too long, and when I dropped it, it landed on my bare knee, and I didn't flinch. I watched it. The skin puckered and went white at the center, and what I remember is not the pain but the fascination: *that's what I look like underneath*.

The Zippo was brass with an engraving of a duck in flight. My father had won it in a poker game in 1974 from a man named either Glenn or Glyn—he told the story both ways. After the bathroom, I put it back in the top drawer of his dresser, nested in the same small canyon it had made between his undershirts. He never knew. He's been dead eleven years and the lighter is in a box in my closet and sometimes I hold it and run my thumb over the duck and I do not flip it open.

That's not the whole truth either.

Sometimes I flip it open.

---

There's a version of this I could make redemptive. I could tell you that the scar taught me something about vulnerability, about the body's quiet willingness to be harmed. I could tell you I sought help, that someone saw the mark and asked and I answered honestly and a door opened onto language and healing and the long project of becoming less opaque to myself.

None of that happened. What happened is that the scar healed badly and hair doesn't grow on that one patch of knee and every summer when I wear shorts I see it and I feel—not shame, not nostalgia, not even recognition exactly. I feel the specific temperature of that bathroom floor. The hex tiles were white with black diamond accents and three of them were cracked near the base of the toilet. The grout was stained the pale amber of weak tea. I can feel them now, those tiles, the cold of them through the thin fabric of my pajama pants—the ones with the sailboats, I was twelve and still wearing pajamas with sailboats—and I am kneeling there watching a piece of college-ruled loose-leaf paper curl into black nothing, and the house is quiet because my mother is at the kitchen sink with her ice cubes and my father is on the porch with his silence and my sister is in her room with her door locked and her music on so low it's just a pulse through the wall, and each of us is alone with the particular ritual we have invented to survive the others, and no one has told us to do these things, and no one will ask us to stop.

The lighter smelled like naphtha and the ghost of my father's thumb. I have never found the words for that second smell—skin-warmed brass, the faint deposit of oil from a human hand repeated over years until it becomes a kind of seasoning. It is the smell of a habit so deep it has altered the chemistry of an object. I know it the way I know my own name. I could pick it out of a thousand lighters. And I will never be able to give it to you.

That is what I mean. That is exactly what I mean. There are things we carry that will not convert to language, that resist the neat alchemy of metaphor, that sit in the body like a coin in a pocket—worn, warm, spent, and ours.
