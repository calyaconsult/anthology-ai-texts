**Prompt:** For a WWII novel, describe the approach to the front line in Northern France from the point of view of the crew of a Sherman tank.

**Approach to the Front Line: A Sherman Tank Crew’s Perspective**
*Northern France, Summer 1944*

The air inside the Sherman reeks of diesel, sweat, and the metallic tang of spent shell casings. Corporal Jenkins, the commander, stands tall in the open hatch, binoculars pressed to his eyes. Below, Private First Class Miller wrestles the steering levers, the tank’s tracks churning mud as they grind through another hedgerow. The *crunch* of splintering wood and the groan of the suspension echo in the cramped hull.

“Easy, Miller—watch the ditch to the left,” Jenkins barks, his voice competing with the roar of the Ford GAA engine. The driver grunts, his knuckles white. Behind him, Private Rossi, the loader, slams a 75mm shell into the breech, his movements mechanical. “AP or HE, Sarge?” he asks, though he already knows the answer. High-explosive for now—save the armor-piercing for Panzers.

The gunner, Sergeant Cole, squints through his periscope, scanning the horizon. “Movement at two o’clock—farmhouse, maybe.” Jenkins swings his binoculars. *A flicker of gray uniforms.* “Krauts,” he mutters. “Cole, keep an eye. Rossi, ready the coax.” The .30 caliber machine gun rattles as Cole tests the trigger.

Outside, the countryside is a patchwork of shell craters and burnt-out vehicles. A knocked-out Panzer IV smolders in a field, its turret askew. The assistant driver, Private Davies, peers through his slit, gripping the bow gun. “Looks like the 2nd Armored came through here,” he says. Jenkins nods. They’ve seen this before—hedgerow after hedgerow, each a potential ambush.

The radio crackles. *“Baker Company, this is HQ—proceed to grid Echo-7. Enemy artillery active. Over.”* Jenkins keys the mic. “Roger. Moving.” He ducks inside, slamming the hatch. The turret whirs as Cole adjusts the gun.

A distant *whump* echoes. “Incoming!” Miller yanks the levers hard. The Sherman lurches sideways as a shell screams past, detonating in a geyser of earth. “Jesus,” Rossi breathes, crossing himself.

Jenkins forces calm into his voice. “Steady. Cole, traverse right—target that treeline.” The gunner spins the turret, the elevation wheel creaking. “Loaded!” Rossi shouts.

“Fire!” The 75mm roars, the hull shuddering. Smoke billows. Through the haze, a German half-track erupts in flames.

They push forward, the front line now a cacophony of machine guns and mortars. Jenkins pops the hatch again, the acrid smell of cordite stinging his nostrils. Ahead, GIs crouch in ditches, waving them onward. The Sherman rumbles past, its shadow stretching over the rubble of a stone wall.

“Almost there, boys,” Jenkins says, though no one hears him over the din. The war is here, in the mud and the noise and the weight of the hatch above him. Somewhere, a sniper’s bullet pings off the armor. Miller flinches but keeps driving.

The front line isn’t a place—it’s a state of mind. And they’re in it now.

**Prompt:** Write the same scene from the point of view of civilians hiding in the farm house.

## Response

**The Farmhouse: A Civilian’s Perspective**  
*Northern France, Summer 1944*  

The walls of the farmhouse shudder as another shell screams overhead. Dust rains from the ceiling, coating the wooden table where Élise Roux huddles with her two children. Her youngest, Luc, whimpers into her apron, his small hands clutching a carved wooden horse. “Shh, *mon petit*,” she whispers, her voice fraying. “They’ll pass soon.”  

Outside, the guttural bark of German orders mixes with the distant grind of tank treads. Élise’s father, Henri, peers through a crack in the boarded window, his face grim. “Americans,” he mutters. “Or British. The Boches are nervous.”  

A chair clatters upstairs—*the soldiers*. For three days, the Germans have occupied the farmhouse, looting the pantry, smashing heirlooms. Élise’s daughter, Claire, flinches at the noise. At fourteen, she’s old enough to understand the danger, her eyes wide as she clutches a kitchen knife behind her back.  

Suddenly, the roar of an engine crescendos. Through the slit in the door, Élise glimpses the hulking shape of a Sherman, its white star stark on the olive drab. A German half-track sputters to life in the courtyard, soldiers scrambling.  

“*Scheiße! Panzer!*” a voice shouts.  

The Sherman’s machine gun rattles, bullets chewing the half-track’s flank. Then, a deafening *boom*—the tank’s main gun fires. The farmhouse rocks; plaster cracks. Luc screams. Élise drags him under the table, shielding him with her body.  

When the smoke clears, the half-track is a twisted inferno. A German soldier lies motionless in the mud, his helmet askew. The Sherman rumbles past, its commander shouting in a language Élise doesn’t recognize.  

Silence falls, thick and sudden. Henri staggers to the door, peering out. “They’re gone,” he says hoarsely. “Both sides.”  

Claire drops the knife, her hands trembling. Élise crawls from under the table, her dress smudged with dust and Luc’s tears. The child points a shaking finger at the dead soldier. “*Maman…*”  

She turns him away, toward the blackened fields. Somewhere, a bird sings—a foolish sound. The war hasn’t ended, but for now, the farmhouse stands. They’re alive.  

But for how long?
