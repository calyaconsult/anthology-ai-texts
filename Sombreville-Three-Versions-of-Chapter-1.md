## Chapter One: The Town That Prayed in the Rain

The train from Calais took two hours to cover a distance a crow could have managed in twenty minutes, but crows had better sense than to fly to Sombreville.

I saw the town before I saw the station — or rather, I saw what the town wanted you to see first: the cathedral. It rose out of the November murk like a drowned man's hand breaking the surface of a canal, two black spires clawing at a sky the colour of dirty dishwater. Everything else huddled beneath it. The slate roofs, the shuttered factories along the river, the cranes of a port that had stopped pretending to be busy sometime around the last war. The whole town seemed to kneel at the cathedral's feet, and I wondered, not for the last time, whether it knelt out of devotion or because someone had broken its legs.

The rain started as I stepped onto the platform. Of course it did.

Gare de Sombreville was a monument to better days — iron latticework, frosted glass, a clock that had stopped at 4:17 and seen no reason to start again. A single porter watched me haul my own case, smoking with the dedication of a man who'd found his life's calling. I was the only passenger who got off. That told me something. In my experience, towns that people only leave are towns with something worth leaving.

My name is Thomas Rake. The card in my wallet says *Enquiries — Discreet and Thorough*, and beneath that, an address in Holborn that sounds more respectable than it looks. Three days earlier, a man named Pemberton-Hughes of Pemberton-Hughes, Askew & Vane had sat me down in an office that smelled of leather and old money and told me, in the careful language of solicitors, that a client of theirs required certain matters in France to be *looked into*.

The matter was a dead archbishop.

Monseigneur Édouard Falconet, Archbishop of Sombreville, aged sixty-three, found on the floor of his study on the morning of the second of November. All Souls' Day, which was either God's little joke or somebody else's. The French police had examined the scene, consulted a doctor, and pronounced it a heart attack with the swiftness of men catching a train. Case closed. Requiescat in pace. Nothing to see.

Except that somebody in London was paying my day rate plus expenses to see it anyway, and Pemberton-Hughes had gone pale and lawyerly when I asked who. *The client's identity is not material to your enquiries, Mr Rake.* Which meant it was material to everything, and I'd find out the hard way, the way I always did.

Outside the station, a taxi idled at the kerb — a black Citroën with rust freckling its wings like liver spots. The driver was a heavyset man with a face carved out of grey soap, and when I told him the Hôtel des Armateurs, he looked at me in the mirror a beat too long.

"English?"

"Guilty."

"Tourist?" The word came out like he'd never had cause to use it before.

"Business."

He grunted and pulled away, and the town began to unspool past the streaked windows. Cobbled streets slick with rain. Shopfronts with their shutters half down at three in the afternoon. A café where old men sat like they'd been left there and forgotten. Every corner had a shrine — the Virgin in a niche, a crucifix over a doorway, plaster saints staring out from behind wet glass with expressions of gentle disappointment. I'd never seen a town so thoroughly watched over. Or watched.

We passed the cathedral square, and the driver crossed himself with one hand without slowing down. Black crepe still hung on the great west doors. Below it, someone had laid flowers, and someone else — or maybe the rain — had scattered them across the steps like an argument.

"The Archbishop," I said, testing the water. "Sad business."

The mirror found me again. The grey soap face gave nothing away, but the silence had edges to it.

"He was a good man," the driver said finally, in the tone people use when they mean something else entirely. "The town mourns."

"Heart attack, I heard."

He looked back at the road. His knuckles were pale on the wheel.

"Monsieur," he said, "in Sombreville, everyone dies of a heart attack. It is a town of weak hearts."

He didn't say another word until we reached the hotel, and when I overpaid him he didn't thank me, just watched me stand there in the rain with my case, the way you'd watch a man wade into a river you knew had currents.

The Hôtel des Armateurs was tall and narrow and leaned slightly toward the harbour, like a drunk trying to hear a whispered conversation. From the window of my room on the third floor, I could see the cathedral spires above the rooftops, black against the failing light, and the rain coming down between us like a curtain that somebody had drawn on purpose.

I unpacked my case. Two shirts, a bottle of scotch, and a copy of the police report that had cost my client — whoever the hell my client was — more than my week's fee.

*Natural causes,* it said.

I poured a drink and looked at the spires until the dark swallowed them.

In my experience, nothing about a dead archbishop is natural.

## Chapter One: The Gray Coast

The train from Paris took three hours, and it rained for all of them.

I watched northern France slide past the window like a film shot through a dirty lens — beet fields drowning in November mud, brick villages the color of dried blood, slag heaps rising out of the fog like the burial mounds of some forgotten industrial god. By the time the conductor called out *Sombreville*, I'd finished my last American cigarette and started wondering what kind of sins a man had to commit in a previous life to get sent here in the off-season.

Not that Sombreville had an on-season.

The station was a cathedral of soot and iron, built back when coal was king and the town had money to burn. Now the great glass roof was patched with plywood, and pigeons held mass in the rafters. My footsteps echoed. Two other passengers got off with me — an old woman in black, and a soldier who looked like he'd rather have stayed on the train.

I couldn't blame him.

My name is Frank Corsaro. I run a two-room agency on Wilshire Boulevard, the kind of operation that pays the rent on divorce work and insurance fraud, and I hadn't crossed an ocean since the Army decided it was done with me in '68. But three days ago, a senior partner at Whitmore, Bass & Delacroix had sat me down in a corner office with a view of the Hollywood Hills and slid a first-class ticket across a desk that cost more than my car.

"The Archbishop of Sombreville is dead," he'd said, like he was reading a weather report. "Monsignor Étienne Vasseur. The French police say heart attack. Natural causes. Case closed."

"So what do you need me for?"

"Our client doesn't believe in natural causes." He'd smiled the way lawyers smile when they're about to tell you nothing. "Our client believes in very little, Mr. Corsaro. Except that the Archbishop was murdered, and that the truth is worth a great deal of money to keep buried — or to dig up. We'd like you to find out which."

"Who's the client?"

"That," he'd said, "is not part of your retainer."

Ten thousand up front. Two hundred a day plus expenses. A dead priest in a town I'd never heard of, and a client without a name. Everything about it smelled wrong, the way week-old fish smells wrong, and I'd taken it anyway. That's the thing about my business. You don't get to choose between good cases and bad ones. You choose between bad ones and the rent.

Outside the station, Sombreville was waiting for me.

The town crouched under a sky the color of wet ash, all narrow streets and blackened brick, sloping down toward a harbor I could smell but not see. Somewhere out there was the Channel, and beyond it England, and beyond that, if you squinted hard enough, the rest of the world. Cranes stood motionless along the waterfront like gallows. Above the rooftops, on a hill at the edge of town, the cathedral rose out of the mist — two Gothic towers, black with two centuries of coal smoke, keeping watch over a flock that had mostly stopped believing.

That's where he died, I thought. Up there in the shadow of God, in a locked study, with his heart supposedly giving out at sixty-one years old.

A taxi idled at the curb, a beat-up Peugeot with one headlight. The driver was reading *L'Équipe* and didn't look up until I knocked on the glass.

"Hôtel des Anglais," I said. My French was Army French, rusted through, but it got the job done.

He looked at me a long moment. Then at my suitcase. Then back at me, and something moved behind his eyes — not curiosity. Something more careful than that.

"American," he said. It wasn't a question.

"That obvious?"

He folded his newspaper slowly, like a man buying time to think. "We do not get Americans in November, monsieur. We do not get *anyone* in November."

"I'm here on business."

"There is no business in Sombreville." He turned the key and the engine coughed to life. "Not anymore. Not since—"

He stopped himself. Put the car in gear.

"Since what?" I asked.

His eyes found mine in the rearview mirror, and for a second I saw it plain as day: fear. The genuine article. The kind you can't fake and can't hide, the kind I'd seen in a hundred faces back home right before they told me something that got somebody killed.

"Since the mines closed," he said flatly, and I knew it was a lie before the sentence was finished.

We drove down toward the sea through streets that emptied ahead of us like the town was holding its breath. In a café window, faces turned to watch the taxi pass. On a church wall, someone had painted words in dripping white letters, and the rain hadn't quite washed them away:

***IL SAVAIT.***

*He knew.*

I lit my last French cigarette from the pack I'd bought in Paris and watched the cathedral towers disappear into the fog above us.

Natural causes, the police said.

Sure. And I was here for the weather.

## Chapter One

The beet fields gave out twenty kilometers before the town did, and after that it was slag heaps — black pyramids furred with birch scrub, the graves of an industry nobody had bothered to bury properly. I drove with the window cracked because the heater in the Peugeot smelled like burning hair, and the air that came in smelled like wet coal and sugar refinery, which meant I was close.

I hadn't needed the road signs. Some geographies you carry in the body. Seven years I'd spent here, from thirty to thirty-seven, and every kilometer of the D643 came back to me the way a bruise comes back when you press on it.

The newspaper was folded on the passenger seat, three days old now, gone soft at the creases from being opened and reopened. *La Voix du Nord*, page eight. **MONSEIGNEUR AUBEL S'ÉTEINT À 71 ANS.** A photograph of the archbishop at some ordination, hands raised, rings catching the flash. The article said he'd been found in his study at the archevêché by his secretary, that the medical examiner had concluded cardiac arrest, that the diocese was in mourning, that the funeral would be celebrated at the cathedral Saturday. Natural causes. Case closed before it was ever open.

The name at the bottom, in the single line about the police having ruled out foul play, was Capitaine Marc Descamps.

Descamps. I'd said it out loud in my kitchen in Lille, standing over the paper with coffee going cold, and my own voice had sounded strange to me. Descamps, who twelve years ago had signed the file on Julien Warnier, twenty-three years old, seminarian, pulled out of the canal Saint-Éloi on a February morning with no water in his lungs and a ruling of suicide stapled to him like a toe tag. Descamps, who had told me — kindly, the way you talk to a dog that won't stop barking — that grief made families see conspiracies, and that ambitious lieutenants who saw them too didn't stay lieutenants long.

He'd been wrong about that. I'd stayed a lieutenant. Just not in Sombreville. The transfer order had come down four months after Aubel's installation as archbishop, and I'd learned later, from a clerk who owed me, that His Excellency had made exactly one phone call to the préfecture that spring. About me.

Now Aubel was dead in his study, and Descamps had signed the paper, and I had six weeks of accumulated leave and a hotel reservation I'd made under my own name because I no longer had any other name to use. No mandate. No client. No badge that meant anything east of Lille. Just a bad heater, a folded newspaper, and a question I'd been carrying for twelve years like a stone in my shoe.

The town announced itself the way it always had: the cathedral first, its single crooked spire rising out of the murk above the rooftops, black with a century of coal smoke that no diocese had ever found the money to sandblast off. Then the canal, running alongside the road, flat and brown and patient. A barge sat rusting against the quay where the sugar used to load. Nothing moved on the water. Nothing had moved on that water for a long time, except once, twelve years ago in February, something had — face down, cassock spread around it like a black flower.

I slowed at the bridge. Old habit. From the bridge you could see the lock, and past the lock the towpath where they'd brought Julien up, and past that the wall of the seminary garden, higher than a wall around a garden needs to be.

Rain started as I came into the centre-ville. Of course it did. In Sombreville the rain wasn't weather, it was architecture — a permanent gray fixture, like the war memorial and the shuttered Monoprix. The Place du Beffroi was half empty at four in the afternoon, cobbles slick, a café with steamed windows, a funeral notice taped inside the door of the tabac. I didn't have to get out to know whose. The whole town was wearing him already. Purple bunting on the cathedral doors. A photograph in the pharmacy window, black ribbon at the corner. Charles-Marie Aubel, who had shepherded this dying town for twelve years and made one phone call about me, smiling out at the square in laminated color.

*You died alone in a locked room,* I told the photograph, *and the man who decided you died naturally is a man I know to be a liar.*

The Hôtel des Flandres was where I remembered it, on the rue Gambetta, three stars that had been generous even in 1998. The woman at the desk was new, young, and didn't know my face, which was one small mercy. She gave me a key to room 14 and asked, out of politeness or boredom, if I was here for the funeral.

"Something like that," I said.

"Terrible thing," she said. "He was a saint, everyone says. But at least it was peaceful. In his sleep, practically. His heart just stopped."

"That's what I read," I said.

I carried my own bag up the stairs. The room looked out over the rooftops toward the cathedral, the spire dissolving into rain and early dark. Somewhere below it, in the archevêché, there was a study that had already been cleaned, aired, emptied of whatever it had held. Somewhere in the commissariat there was a file two pages thick with Descamps's signature at the bottom, the second time in twelve years he'd signed away a body I had questions about.

I hung my coat on the door, sat on the edge of the bed, and unfolded the newspaper one more time.

Heart attacks happen. Men of seventy-one die at their desks every day, and grief makes people see conspiracies, and ambitious lieutenants who see them too don't stay lieutenants long.

I'd had twelve years to become an unambitious one.

Outside, the bells of the cathedral began to toll the hour, slow and cracked, over the black water and the rain, and I sat in the dark of room 14 and let Sombreville have me back.
