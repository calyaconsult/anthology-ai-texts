**Prompt:** For a WWII novel, describe the approach to the front line in Northern France from the point of view of the crew of a Sherman tank.

## Response

# Approach to the Front

The Sherman lurched forward through another pothole, and Corporal Jake Miller felt the jolt travel up through the driver's seat into his spine. He'd stopped noticing them individually about two miles back—now they were just part of the constant vibration that turned his bones to jelly and his teeth into castanets.

"Driver, easy on the left track," Lieutenant Brennan's voice crackled through the intercom from his commander's position in the turret. "We're getting close."

"Yes, sir," Miller responded, though "easy" was a relative term when you were piloting thirty tons of steel through Norman mud. The engine roared behind the firewall, drowning out everything except the clank and squeal of the tracks. The diesel fumes were thick enough to taste, mixing with the smell of gun oil, sweat, and the metallic tang of the .75mm shells stacked beside Kowalski, their loader.

Through his periscope, Miller could see the landscape had changed. The pastoral fields they'd rolled through at dawn had given way to something uglier. Shell craters pocked the earth like sores. A burned-out half-track sat canted at an angle in a ditch, American markings still visible through the carbon scoring. He'd learned not to look too closely at wrecks like that.

"Hedgerow coming up," he reported. "Twenty yards."

"I see it," Brennan replied. The hedgerows—the French called them bocage—were earthen walls six feet high, packed with centuries of roots and topped with thick brush. Natural fortifications that turned every field into a potential killing box. They'd already lost two Shermans from the company to German anti-tank guns hidden in these damned hedgerows.

Miller slowed, feeling the tank's weight settle. Sergeant Fischer, their gunner, would be traversing the turret now, scanning for threats. Miller could picture him hunched over his sights, hand on the traverse wheel, the other on the firing mechanism. Fischer never said much, but the man could put a shell through a barn door at a thousand yards.

"Hold here," Brennan ordered.

The Sherman shuddered to a stop. In the sudden relative quiet—the engine still thundering, but at least they weren't moving—Miller could hear the distant crump of artillery. Friendly, he hoped. Maybe the 105s they'd heard were set up behind the ridge.

"Command net's reporting enemy armor, two clicks northeast," Brennan said, his voice tight. "Looks like we're walking into it."

Miller's hands tightened on the controls. Enemy armor meant Panthers, maybe Tigers. The Sherman's 75mm could hurt them if you got lucky, caught them in the flank, hit the thinner armor. Head-on? You might as well throw rocks. The boys had nicknamed their tank "Ronson" after the cigarette lighter—lights first time, every time. Dark humor for a dark truth: Shermans burned when they were hit.

"Tommy, you awake down there?" That was Brennan addressing Private Thomas Greene, their assistant driver and bow gunner, squeezed into the hull beside Miller.

"Awake and scared shitless, sir," Greene replied. A couple of nervous laughs rippled through the intercom. Greene was nineteen, on his second week at the front. He handled the .30 caliber machine gun and served as extra eyes.

"Good," Brennan said. "Scared keeps you sharp. All right, listen up. We're pushing through this hedgerow with Third Platoon. Artillery's supposed to have softened up the German positions, but you know how that goes. Watch for panzerfaust teams in the hedges. Fischer, you see anything that looks like a gun emplacement, you light it up. Kowalski, keep those shells coming. We stop, we die. Everybody got it?"

A chorus of "Yes, sirs" came back.

"Miller, take us through. Slow and steady."

Miller eased the throttle forward. The Sherman's engine growled, and the tracks bit into the churned earth. They hit the hedgerow at an angle, the way they'd been taught—the tank modified with salvaged steel welded to the front, crude tusks that let them rip through the root-packed walls instead of climbing them and exposing their thin belly armor.

The Sherman bucked and heaved. Branches scraped against the hull like fingernails on a coffin. Earth showered down on the front slope. Then they were through, bursting into another field.

"Contact left!" Greene shouted.

Miller's heart jumped into his throat. Through his periscope he caught a glimpse of movement—figures in field gray, maybe two hundred yards out, scrambling along a treeline.

"Traverse left!" Brennan barked. "Fischer, you got 'em?"

"Got 'em."

"HE round!"

"HE up!" Kowalski slammed the high-explosive shell into the breech.

The 75mm roared, the concussion wave slamming through the crew compartment even through their headsets. The smell of cordite was immediate and overwhelming. Miller kept the tank moving, not waiting to see if they'd hit. You never stopped. Stopping made you a target.

"Short!" Fischer reported. "Reloading—"

Something cracked past them, a sound like God ripping canvas. Miller flinched instinctively. A geyser of dirt erupted thirty feet to their right.

"Anti-tank gun!" Brennan's voice had gone cold, professional. "Two o'clock, in that barn! Fischer—"

"Identified!"

"AP round!"

"AP up!" The armor-piercing shell was already sliding into the breech. Kowalski was good.

The gun fired again, and this time Miller saw the barn's wall explode outward in a shower of splinters and dust. Secondary explosions rippled through the structure—ammunition cooking off.

"Hit! Good shooting, Fischer."

But there was no time to celebrate. Tracers were stitching across their path now, small arms fire pinging off the armor like hail. Miller saw infantry rising from a network of trenches ahead—a whole defensive line they were about to drive into.

"Buttoned up!" Brennan ordered. Miller heard the commander's hatch clang shut above. Now they were truly blind except for their periscopes, sealed in their steel coffin, dependent on narrowed vision and each other's eyes.

The machine guns opened up, Greene's .30 caliber chattering, the coaxial gun beside Fischer's position adding its own voice. The world contracted to noise and vibration, to the narrow field of view through thick glass, to the voice of the lieutenant calling targets and the mechanical ballet of loader and gunner working in the cramped turret.

More Shermans were coming through the hedgerow behind them now—Miller caught glimpses in his periscope. The whole platoon advancing in a ragged line, their infantry support somewhere in the chaos, though he couldn't see them.

A German soldier appeared directly in front of the tank, panzerfaust tube on his shoulder. Miller jerked the controls, felt the tank slew sideways. Greene's machine gun hammered. The German went down, the panzerfaust firing wild, its rocket streaking overhead with a banshee wail.

"Jesus Christ!" Greene's voice was shaking.

"Keep firing!" Brennan commanded. "Driver, forward! Through them!"

Miller pushed the throttle. This was it, the final approach to the German line. This was what it all came down to—not strategy or tactics, but simply rolling forward into the guns, trusting your armor, trusting your crew, trusting that you'd punch through before something punched through you.

The trench line was fifty yards away. Thirty. Muzzle flashes sparkled along it like deadly stars. Something heavier hit them—probably a panzerschreck—and the whole tank rang like a bell, but the armor held. Miller tasted blood where he'd bitten his tongue.

"They're running!" Greene shouted. "The infantry's breaking!"

And they were—field-gray figures streaming back from the trenches, abandoning their positions as the American armor bore down on them. The front line, such as it was, collapsing.

But Miller knew better than to feel relief. This was just the approach. Beyond this field would be another hedgerow, another defensive position. And somewhere out there were the real killers, the Panthers and Tigers, waiting in hull-down positions with their long 75mm and 88mm guns.

"Keep moving," Brennan ordered, and Miller could hear the exhaustion creeping into the lieutenant's voice. "Command says there's a crossroads half a click ahead. That's our objective."

Half a click. Five hundred meters of Norman hell.

Miller gripped the controls tighter and drove on, deeper into the guns, the Sherman's engine roaring defiance, its tracks chewing up the contested earth. Around him, his crew worked with the fluid efficiency of men who knew that their survival depended on each other—Fischer traversing to new targets, Kowalski feeding the gun, Greene scanning for threats, Brennan coordinating it all.

Outside, the war hammered on. Inside, five men fought their own private battle, sealed in steel and fumes and fear, approaching the front one bloody field at a time.
