# Sample Chapter: Chapter 7 – A False Note

---

The dinner in the small dining room of Thalbury Hall was drawing to a close. Candlelight fell warmly upon the heavy silver cutlery, upon the arrangements of white roses, and upon the faces of the few guests whom Prince Constantin had invited to this first, ostensibly informal evening of introduction.

Countess Božena von Šternberg-Kolowrat sat at his right hand and smiled.

She had been smiling since she entered the room — a quiet, almost self-effacing smile that lent her even features an air of composed grace. Her gown, midnight-blue velvet with a hem of old Brussels lace, was faultlessly chosen. Not too splendid, not too modest. Everything about her seemed measured, as though an invisible hand had carefully staged every detail.

Constantin had to admit that Viktor had done a thorough job. The Countess was beautiful, possessed of that dark, Slavonic cast of feature which had once broken so many hearts in the salons of the old Dual Monarchy. Her conversation was polished. She spoke a soft, melodious German with a faint accent that might have recalled Prague — old palaces on the Malá Strana, fading coats of arms above baroque portals.

And yet.

It was nothing Constantin could have named. A shadow — not even that — a draught of air passing across the smooth surface of her manner, leaving behind, for the merest fraction of a second, a ripple.

The old Dowager Lady Wetherstone, Constantin's great-aunt and for fifty years the unofficial guardian of protocol, had steered the conversation towards the summer months — the habits of old families, life in the country, the shooting.

"My late father," said the Dowager, dabbing the corners of her mouth with her napkin, "used to say that one knows the true character of a person by how they conduct themselves in September at a country house, when the last guest has departed and only the dogs remain for company."

A soft ripple of laughter passed through the company.

"How true," said Božena, inclining her head. "Our family always spent the summers in the country. Koschitz Castle in southern Bohemia — you may know it, perhaps, Lady Wetherstone? It lies not far from Budweis, on the edge of the Bohemian Forest. My grandfather kept a splendid pack of Brandlbracken."

"Brandlbracken?" The Dowager raised her brows. "In Bohemia?"

"A passion of my grandfather's," Božena replied, without hesitation. "He had them brought from the Tyrol."

Constantin noticed how Viktor, seated at the far end of the table, gave an almost imperceptible nod, as if to confirm the accuracy of this information. It was a small gesture, but it was there.

The Dowager appeared satisfied. The conversation turned to other matters. They spoke of the restoration of the Hall's chapel, of a forthcoming charity gala in London, of whether the stud at Donauwörth would be putting foals up for auction again this year.

It was the moment at which the servants brought in the pudding — a crème brûlée served on old Meissen porcelain — that it happened.

Anna, the young lady's maid who had been assigned to Božena for the duration of her stay, approached the Countess and whispered something in her ear. A trifle, as it later transpired — a question about her choice of breakfast for the following morning. Božena turned half aside and answered quietly. But not quietly enough.

Constantin, seated beside her, heard it.

"Yeah, that's fine. Coffee, but make it strong. And them rolls from today was rock hard — you can tell the kitchen that an' all. And a newspaper, but not some rubbish rag like what they give me this morning."

They were only a few words. Spoken hastily, in the tone of that casual impatience with which a person speaks who is accustomed to holding her own in a world quite different from this one. The soft Prague-inflected German had vanished. In its place, something hard and common had broken through — a London accent, an East End accent, abraded but unmistakable: the language of back streets and tenement blocks, not of castles on the edge of the Bohemian Forest.

In the next instant, it was over.

Božena turned back to the table. The smile returned, flawless, as though someone had straightened a mask that had slipped for the space of a heartbeat. She reached for her dessert spoon, broke the caramelised crust of the crème brûlée with a perfectly executed little gesture — from the outside inward, as etiquette required — and said to the Dowager:

"Forgive me, Lady Wetherstone — you were speaking earlier of the gardens at Blenheim. My grandmother on my mother's side, a Kinsky by birth, had a great fondness for English landscape gardening…"

The Dowager smiled, flattered, and allowed herself to be drawn into a conversation about box hedges and orangeries.

Constantin said nothing.

He had not yet touched his spoon. His gaze rested on the profile of the woman beside him, on the flawless line of her neck, on the garnet earrings that looked so convincingly like old Bohemian family heirlooms.

*You can tell the kitchen that an' all.*

Perhaps he had misheard. Perhaps it was nothing. People sometimes spoke differently to servants than to their own kind — that was no disgrace, merely habit, nothing more.

And yet.

He sought Viktor's eye at the far end of the table. But Viktor was deep in conversation with the Earl of Seebach and did not look his way.

Or would not look his way.

Constantin took up his spoon and tasted the crème brûlée. It was excellent. He praised it with a few words to Božena, who thanked him gracefully, and the conversation rippled on like a river whose surface betrayed nothing of the currents pulling in the deep.

Later, when the guests had withdrawn to the drawing room and Božena was playing a Chopin nocturne at the grand piano — technically impeccable, but curiously devoid of feeling, Constantin thought, as though she had learnt the notes by heart without ever having touched the meaning of the music — the old Dowager appeared at his side.

She said nothing. She merely laid her wrinkled hand upon his arm and pressed it, very lightly, and looked at him with those watchful eyes that in eighty years of life had overlooked very little.

Then she said, so softly that only he could hear:

"Brandlbracken, my boy, are not pack hounds. One hunts with them singly, or in pairs. Your grandfather knew that. Anyone who has ever hunted with hounds knows that."

She patted his arm and moved away.

At the piano, Božena played Chopin, and her smile was so perfect that it almost hurt to look upon it.

But Constantin stood at the window, gazing out into the darkness of the park, and thought of a pair of bright eyes that had smiled at him three days ago in the university library — artless, without calculation, without a mask.

He thought of Katharina.

And for the first time since Viktor had spoken the name Šternberg-Kolowrat, he allowed himself the word he had refused to think until this evening:

*A lie.*
